How to Read a Balance Sheet

The Core Equation

Every balance sheet is built on one identity that never breaks:

Assets = Liabilities + Shareholders' Equity

This means everything the company owns (assets) was funded either by borrowing (liabilities) or by the owners/retained profits (equity). If a company has $10B in assets and $6B in debt, the other $4B is the equity "cushion" protecting shareholders.

The Three Sections

  • Current Assets — Cash, accounts receivable, inventory. Things that will convert to cash within 12 months. This is your "quick liquidity" pool.
  • Non-Current Assets — Property, equipment, patents, goodwill. Long-term investments that generate value over years. Goodwill is an intangible premium paid in acquisitions — worth watching.
  • Current Liabilities — Bills due within 12 months (payables, short-term debt). If current liabilities exceed current assets, the company may struggle to meet near-term obligations (Current Ratio below 1.0).
  • Long-Term Liabilities — Debt due beyond 12 months. Manageable in stable businesses; dangerous in cyclical or high-rate environments.
  • Shareholders' Equity — What's left for owners. Retained earnings (cumulative net profits kept in the business) is one of the most important line items here — growing retained earnings over time is a hallmark of a great business.

Key Ratios to Watch

  • Current Ratio = Current Assets ÷ Current Liabilities — above 1.5 is healthy.
  • Debt-to-Equity = Total Debt ÷ Equity — below 1.0 preferred; high D/E amplifies risk.
  • Book Value Per Share = Total Equity ÷ Shares Outstanding — the accounting floor for share price.
How to Read an Income Statement

The Profit Waterfall

The income statement is a journey from total revenue down to the profit shareholders actually keep. Each line subtracts a different category of cost:

Revenue (Net Sales) $10,000
− Cost of Goods Sold (COGS) $4,000
= Gross Profit $6,000
− Operating Expenses (SG&A, R&D) $2,000
= Operating Income (EBIT) $4,000
− Interest Expense & Taxes $1,200
= Net Income $2,800

Margin Analysis

  • Gross Margin = Gross Profit ÷ Revenue — reveals pricing power and production efficiency. High and stable is good.
  • Operating Margin = Operating Income ÷ Revenue — shows how efficient management is at running the core business before financing and taxes.
  • Net Margin = Net Income ÷ Revenue — the bottom line: how many cents of profit per dollar of revenue. Trending up over time is the signal.
  • EPS (Earnings Per Share) = Net Income ÷ Diluted Shares — what each share "earned." Consistent EPS growth drives long-term price appreciation.

Why Net Income Can Mislead

Net income is an accounting concept. It includes non-cash items (depreciation, amortization), and management has discretion over when revenue and expenses are recognized. A company can show positive net income while actually running out of cash — which is why you always cross-check with the cash flow statement.

How to Read a Cash Flow Statement

Three Sections

  • Operating Cash Flow (OCF) — Cash generated by the core business. This is the most important number. A business with strong, growing OCF is a real business, not an accounting trick. It starts from net income and adds back non-cash items (depreciation) and working capital changes.
  • Investing Cash Flow — Cash spent on acquiring assets, investments, or acquisitions (usually negative for growing companies). Capital Expenditures (CapEx) live here — the price of maintaining and expanding physical operations.
  • Financing Cash Flow — Cash from issuing/repaying debt, issuing/buying back stock, and paying dividends. Watch for large, consistent positive financing CF — it often means the company is borrowing to fund operations it can't fund on its own.

Free Cash Flow — Why It's the Gold Standard

Free Cash Flow = Operating Cash Flow − Capital Expenditures

FCF is the cash a company generates after paying for everything needed to keep the business running. It's what the company can actually return to shareholders or reinvest in growth — without needing new debt or diluting investors. Buffett calls it "owner earnings."

When FCF ≥ Net Income, reported profits are backed by real cash (high earnings quality). When FCF < Net Income, profits may be driven by accounting adjustments rather than cash changing hands — a warning sign.

Return Metrics Built from These Statements

  • ROE (Return on Equity) = Net Income ÷ Equity — how much profit the company generates for each dollar of shareholder equity. Above 15% consistently is a hallmark of quality businesses. Caution: high ROE from high debt can be misleading — pair with ROIC.
  • ROIC (Return on Invested Capital) = NOPAT ÷ (Equity + Debt) — the most rigorous profitability measure. It shows how efficiently the company uses ALL the capital deployed in it, regardless of how it's financed. ROIC above its cost of capital means the company is creating value; below it means it's destroying value even with positive net income.

Scorecard Verdict Guide

  • 34–40 pts Great Company — Exceptional fundamentals across all four pillars. Strong moat candidate.
  • 26–33 pts Good — Solid fundamentals with minor weaknesses. Worth a closer look for long-term holding.
  • 18–25 pts Caution — Mixed picture. Some areas of strength but material weaknesses present. Requires deeper due diligence.
  • Below 18 Avoid — Significant fundamental deterioration. Not suitable for swing trading or long-term holds without a clear turnaround thesis.
